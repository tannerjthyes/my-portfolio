Starting a blog without a plan

I originally built this site to learn hosting a website on the cloud and to learn fun features I could implement with AWS1. Adding a blog section felt like a natural next step. The only catch is that I am not entirely sure what I all want to include on the blog.

If you have not read my initial landing page, or if it has changed since this was posted, I am Tanner. I studied computer engineering and economics at Purdue and Butler, and I have worked as a software engineer doing both front end design and backend service creation. In my free time I find myself tweaking my Linux setup with Hyprland and Neovim2 to promote keyboard driven efficiency and studying for AWS certifications. Away from the keyboard I enjoy many other interests. I like playing golf, strategic board games, photography, optimizing credit card and finance systems3, journaling, among many more.

Earlier in 2026, I spent 6 months backpacking solo across the world. I came home with a scuba certification and a camera roll full of photos I still need to fully sort through4.

What might end up here

That trip is part of why I wanted a place to post photos and write a few things down. I keep a personal journal, though most of what goes in there is too private to put on the open internet. Running a public blog mostly means figuring out where that line sits.

For now, this space will probably hold a mix of side hobbies and travel stories, along with the occasional technical note. Some posts might cover a hiking trail or a notebook setup I like, while others might just be notes from a weekend project and what I have learned from it.

There is no posting schedule here. When something feels worth sharing, I will put it up. For now I think I will keep it in html structure, but I may convert to a .md HUGO framework5 in the future to make posting more efficient especially if I export it from my Obsidian notebook6.

  1. Amazon Web Services. Using enterprise cloud infrastructure to serve a static personal site is roughly equivalent to renting a freight train to deliver a postcard, though it does double as decent exam prep. ↩︎
  2. Hyprland is a dynamic tiling Wayland compositor, and Neovim is a hyperextensible terminal text editor. Together they let you spend three hours editing config files so you can save four seconds of mouse movement later. ↩︎
  3. Mostly an excuse to turn normal everyday purchases into a spreadsheet exercise and keep tabs on airline transfer partners. ↩︎
  4. A polite way of saying there are several thousand RAW files sitting in a folder that I keep promising myself I will organize "this weekend." ↩︎
  5. Hugo is a static site generator written in Go that compiles plain Markdown (.md) files into HTML pages. Rebuilding a blog's publishing pipeline instead of actually writing posts is a time-honored software engineering tradition. ↩︎
  6. Obsidian is a local-first Markdown note-taking app where everything lives as plain text files in a folder on your machine. Exporting directly from the same place I already write notes sounds almost suspiciously convenient. ↩︎
